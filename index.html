Is the view worth the walk?

Last year people made 65.6 million visits to New South Wales (NSW) national parks, and many of them walked to a lookout. This story compares those lookouts and walking tracks: how popular they are, how hard they are to reach, and what they mean for tourism.

Every two years, NSW National Parks and Wildlife Service (NPWS) counts how many visits are made to its parks. Visits crept along until 2014, then jumped.

COVID-19 lockdowns cut about 10 million visits in 2020. Numbers climbed back in 2022, and by 2024/25 they passed the pre-COVID peak to reach a new record.

Where do people go, and where are the views?

NPWS runs its parks in eight regions. Greater Sydney is the smallest of them, yet its parks get about 28 million visits a year, more than any other region. 8 of the 20 busiest parks are in it. How crowded are the lookouts?

Lookouts are spread unevenly across NSW

NPWS records 794 lookouts across its parks. The Blue Mountains has the most (195), while Greater Sydney has just 55 — despite being the region with the most park visits.

To compare visitation with lookout numbers, I divided each region's park visits in 2024/25 by its number of lookouts. Greater Sydney had around 514,000 visits per lookout, compared with about 12,000 in Northern Inland.

And the pull of Sydney keeps growing

Each small map below is one survey. The darker a region, the bigger its share of all park visits that year. Greater Sydney is the small dark patch on the coast: tiny on the map, but it gets the most visits.

In 2008 Greater Sydney had 30% of all park visits. By 2024/25 it had 43%, more than 4 in every 10 visits. The biggest jump came in 2020, the COVID-19 year, when its share rose from 33% to 39%.

Almost every other region lost ground. The Blue Mountains, Hunter Central Coast, South Coast, North Coast, Southern Ranges and Northern Inland all ended up with a smaller share than in 2008. The one exception is the West: still small, but up from 1% to 4%.

How hard is it to get to the view?

NPWS grades every walking track from Class 1, an all-access path, up to Class 6, an unmarked route you find your own way along. To make that easier to picture, we grouped the six classes into three: easy, moderate and hard.

Imagine all the walking track in NSW national parks shrunk to 100 squares. Only 7% would be easy walking, 69% moderate and 24% hard going.

It depends on the park

Where you go makes a big difference. At Kamay Botany Bay, 69% of the track is easy walking. At Morton, 87% is hard. And the most visited park of all, Blue Mountains, is one of the tougher ones: 37% of its track is hard and only 4% is easy.

Easy walking is not what draws the crowds. In 18 of the 20 busiest parks, less than a quarter of the track is easy.

Who goes to the parks, and where do they come from?

Most park visitors live close by. People from Sydney made 42.1 million visits in 2024/25 (64% of all visits) and people from the rest of NSW another 12.7 million. The other 10.8 million visits, about one in six, came from interstate. Melbourne, regional Queensland and Brisbane each sent around two million, and Canberra, just over the border, another 1.7 million.

And they come from overseas too

National parks are also one of the main things international tourists do in NSW. 72% of international visitors to the state, about 2.8 million people, went to at least one NPWS park. A third of them visited more than one. China, South Korea and the United States sent the most. These visitors are not part of the 65.6 million domestic visits above.

Hover a circle for the exact number of visitors.

For locals the park is the trip. For interstate visitors it's one stop

Splitting visitors into these two groups explains most of how they use the parks. For 70% of NSW visitors the park was the only or main reason for the trip, and 88% went home the same day. For interstate visitors only 28% came mainly for the park, but 28% stayed at least one night. That is where parks help tourism: the park is one part of a longer trip that brings accommodation, meals and other spending. Both groups left happy, although NSW visitors were a little more satisfied (90% against 85%).

When do people go?

Parks are busy all year round. Most months are within about 5% of an average month, but the summer holidays stand out: December was 11% busier than average and January 9%. Easter gave April a smaller lift.

Why do they go?

Walking is the most common thing people do in a national park, and touring and sightseeing comes second. That is the lookout story: most visitors come to walk somewhere and look at the view. Photography makes the top ten as well.

And they come home happy

89% of visitors were satisfied with their last park visit, and more than half (55%) were very satisfied. NSW visitors were a little happier than interstate visitors (90% against 85%). Visitors aged 50 to 64 were the most satisfied (94%), and 18 to 34 year olds the least (85%), though still a large majority.

The view from the end of the trail

So, is the view worth the walk? The data points to a growing interest in NSW's national parks, but that interest is not spread evenly across the state. Greater Sydney attracts a large share of visits, while the accessibility of walking tracks varies considerably. The appeal of these landscapes is clear, but the opportunity to experience them is not the same everywhere.

There is also an important distinction between visiting a national park and visiting a lookout. Park visitation figures cannot tell us exactly how crowded individual lookouts are, and visits per lookout should be treated as a broad regional comparison rather than a direct measure of crowding.

These findings point to two considerations for park management: making natural experiences more accessible and understanding where visitor demand places the greatest pressure on park facilities. The goal is not simply to bring more people to the parks, but to help them experience these landscapes sustainably.
